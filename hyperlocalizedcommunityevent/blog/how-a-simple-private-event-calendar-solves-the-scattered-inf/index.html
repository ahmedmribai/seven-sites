Why Existing Apps Create Technical Frustration

While many mainstream event apps promise a comprehensive solution, their feature-rich design often introduces a host of technical frustrations that ultimately discourage use, particularly for residents in small towns. Users frequently encounter issues like unexpected crashes or apps freezing mid-scroll, turning what should be a quick check into a frustrating ordeal. This isn't just an inconvenience; a slow-loading app, especially over inconsistent rural internet connections, can mean missing out on critical event details or giving up on the search entirely. The constant background activity and complex interfaces required by these full-featured platforms also contribute significantly to battery drain, leaving users with a dead phone when they need it most.

Beyond performance, the sheer volume of features in these apps often necessitates extensive data collection. This raises legitimate privacy concerns among users, who worry about how their personal data and local activity information are being handled and shared. The perceived trade-off - sacrificing privacy for a feature set they don't fully utilize - erodes trust and makes them hesitant to engage. Even when these apps function as intended, they frequently fail to centralize truly local information effectively. Events remain scattered across various platforms, from community Facebook groups to local library websites, forcing residents to juggle multiple sources. The promise of a single solution is undermined by technical friction and a lack of focus on the specific, simple need for reliable local event awareness, pushing users back to less efficient but less frustrating methods.

Mainstream event services such as Facebook Events, Eventbrite, and Nextdoor collect a broad set of personal details when users create an account or RSVP to an activity. This includes obvious identifiers like name, email, and phone number, as well as less visible data such as IP‑derived location, device fingerprints, and the list of friends or groups a user belongs to. The platforms then use this information to build advertising profiles: Facebook, for example, lets advertisers target “Custom Audiences” built from event RSVPs, while Eventbrite shares attendee lists with event organizers and may pass aggregated data to marketing partners for promotional emails. Nextdoor’s business model includes offering local businesses access to neighborhood‑specific insights derived from user activity. In addition to direct sharing, these companies often enter data‑licensing deals with third‑party brokers who resell or enrich the information for credit scoring, insurance underwriting, or political micro‑targeting. Because the data flow is opaque and the user rarely sees exactly who receives their information, residents of small towns - who value tight‑knit community trust and minimal surveillance - feel uneasy. They worry that their participation in a local fair or a library talk could be harvested for unrelated commercial or political purposes, eroding confidence in the platform and prompting a preference for solutions that keep data under their own control.

A Minimal‑Tech Solution: Static Site + Daily JSON Update

A static site hosted on a free tier such as Netlify or GitHub Pages forms the backbone of the MVP. Each night a lightweight scraper - running as a GitHub Actions workflow or a cheap cron job on a $5 USD VPS - visits the town’s official calendar, the public library’s events page, and a handful of community‑group sites that already publish HTML tables or iCal feeds. The scraper extracts the title, date, time, and location for each upcoming event, normalises the data into a single JSON file (events.json), and commits the update to the repository. The static site then reads this JSON at build time, generating a plain‑HTML list that can be filtered by date or category with a few lines of vanilla JavaScript. Because the site consists only of HTML, CSS, a small JSON payload, and minimal script, there is no database, no server‑side rendering, and no third‑party SDKs that could introduce points of failure.

This architecture directly addresses the crash and slowdown complaints. With no moving parts on the server, the only thing that can fail is the nightly scraper, which runs independently of the user experience and can be retried or alerted without affecting the live site. Visitors receive a pre‑built HTML file served from a CDN; the total payload is typically under 50 KB, meaning the page loads in under a second even on a 3G connection and remains usable offline after the initial fetch. Since the client never executes heavy frameworks or makes repeated API calls, the likelihood of freezing or long‑running scripts is eliminated.

Privacy is preserved by design. No user accounts are created, and no personal data is collected beyond the optional email or phone number a visitor supplies for a daily reminder - handled through a lightweight third‑party service such as Buttondown or Twilio, which stores only the address and a timestamp. The scraper accesses only publicly available information, and the JSON file contains no identifiers beyond the event details themselves. Consequently, residents gain a reliable, fast, and privacy‑respecting source for local happenings without the technical fragility or data‑harvesting concerns of mainstream platforms.

How Data Is Handled to Protect Your Privacy

1. DECOMPOSE

- Identify what data the service collects and where it originates.

- Determine how to store that data without retaining any personal identifiers.

- Define the mechanism for optional notifications (email/SMS) that respects user choice.

- Specify how users can easily withdraw consent and what happens to their contact information upon opt‑out.

- Outline the transparency measures needed to demonstrate privacy compliance to residents.

2. SYNTHESIZE

The scraper runs each night at 02:00 UTC, pulling only fields that are already published on the town’s official calendar, the library’s events page, and a few community‑group sites: event title, start‑end time, venue address, and a brief description. These fields are written to a single JSON file (events.json) that replaces the previous version; no user‑generated content, login credentials, or tracking cookies are ever written to disk. The file is hosted on the same static‑site bucket as the public calendar, so anyone can inspect its contents directly.

For residents who want a reminder, the landing page offers two clearly labeled toggles: “Get tomorrow’s events by email” and “Get tomorrow’s events by SMS.” Selecting either opens a minimal form that asks only for an email address or a phone number. Submission triggers a double‑opt‑in workflow: a confirmation message containing a unique, time‑limited link (or code) must be clicked or replied to before the address is added to the mailing list. The list itself is stored in a separate, access‑controlled file (subscribers.txt) that contains only the hashed contact information; the raw address is never logged or backed up.

Opting out is equally simple: each reminder includes an unsubscribe link that, when clicked, immediately removes the corresponding hash from subscribers.txt and sends a final confirmation that no further messages will be sent. Because the service never stores names, preferences, or browsing history, the only data that could be linked to an individual is the contact information they voluntarily provide, and that link is severed as soon as they opt out.

3. CRITIQUE

Weakest step: The assumption that users will trust the double‑opt‑in process without a clear, visible privacy notice may lead to low conversion or suspicion.

Repair: Place a concise privacy notice directly beneath the subscription toggles, stating in plain language that only publicly scraped event data is stored, that personal contact information is used solely for the chosen reminder channel, and that users can view or delete their information at any time via the unsubscribe link. Link the notice to a dedicated, static privacy page that outlines the data flow, retention period (subscriber hashes are kept only until opt‑out, after which they are purged), and contact details for questions. This added transparency reduces uncertainty and reinforces the opt‑out guarantee.

The service therefore handles data by limiting collection to already‑public event specifics, storing nothing personal unless a user explicitly opts in for reminders, and providing an immediate, verifiable way to withdraw that consent. Nightly updates keep the events.json file lightweight - typically under 10 KB for a town with fifteen daily events - while the subscriber list remains a simple, encrypted‑hash file that is purged the moment a user opts out. This approach delivers timely local event information without compromising the privacy expectations of small‑town residents.

Getting Started: Subscribe and See Tomorrow’s Events

Visit the calendar at https://your‑town‑events.example.com on any phone or computer. The page loads as a single static file, so it opens instantly even on a slow 3G connection and never crashes because there is no heavy JavaScript or backend to fail. When the page opens you see a prompt to enter your five‑digit ZIP code; type 12345 and press Enter. The site then reads a JSON file that was refreshed the previous night and displays a clean, mobile‑friendly list of events happening tomorrow in that area. For a typical small town the list might show four items: a farmer’s market at 8 a.m., a library story hour at 10 a.m., a town council meeting at 6 p.m., and a live‑music set at the community center at 8 p.m. Each entry includes the title, time, location, and a one‑sentence description, allowing you to scan quickly without scrolling through unrelated posts or ads.

If you want a reminder each morning, tap the “Subscribe for daily updates” button beneath the list. You can choose email or SMS; entering your address or phone number adds you to a simple mailing list that receives a plain‑text message at 7 a.m. with the same filtered list. No account is created, no personal data is stored beyond the address you provide, and you can unsubscribe with a single link in any message. Because the site only pulls publicly posted event information from the town website, library calendar, and a few community group pages, there is no tracking, no profiling, and no risk of your data being sold or leaked. This approach solves the three pains you described: it eliminates technical glitches by staying lightweight, protects privacy by collecting only what you voluntarily give, and consolidates scattered event notices into one reliable, zip‑code‑specific view.
