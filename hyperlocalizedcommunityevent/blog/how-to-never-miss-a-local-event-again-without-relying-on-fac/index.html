How Small Towns Solved This Decades Ago (But Forgot)

Before the internet, small towns relied on physical bulletin boards and community calendars to keep residents informed about local events. These solutions were simple but effective—centralized, trusted, and accessible to everyone. Bulletin boards, often found at libraries, post offices, or town halls, displayed flyers and announcements in one convenient location. Community calendars, published in local newspapers or distributed as printed pamphlets, compiled events into a single, easy-to-reference list. The core benefit of these systems was their **trusted, centralized nature**: residents knew exactly where to look for information, and the sources were curated by local institutions or community leaders.

Today, these pre-internet solutions have largely been forgotten, replaced by fragmented digital platforms that often fail to meet the same needs. Facebook groups, while popular, bury events in a sea of unrelated posts. Event aggregators like Eventbrite or Meetup are too broad, focusing on larger cities or niche interests. Meanwhile, physical flyers are still used but are easily missed or outdated. The result is a loss of the simplicity and reliability that once defined local event awareness.

The key lesson from these pre-internet solutions is the importance of **centralization and trust**. Residents seek a straightforward way to stay informed about local happenings without navigating multiple sources. This product addresses that by aggregating events from select local sources, such as town websites, libraries, and community groups, and presenting them in a clean, mobile-friendly format. It focuses on providing a simple, centralized view of nearby events, designed to meet the specific needs of small-community residents.

By revisiting these decades-old solutions, we can build a modern alternative that combines the simplicity of bulletin boards with the convenience of digital tools—without the technical headaches, privacy concerns, or scattered information that plague current platforms.

Privacy by Design: No Tracking, No Accounts

**Privacy by Design: No Tracking, No Accounts**

The simplest way to avoid collecting personal data is not to ask for it in the first place. This tool works without signups, accounts, or tracking—just a static webpage that pulls public event listings from local sources (town hall bulletins, library calendars, community boards) and displays them in one place. No cookies, no analytics beyond counting total visitors, and no persistent identifiers. For users who want reminders, a single-field SMS or email opt-in sends a daily digest, with the option to unsubscribe instantly via reply. Data flows one way: the scraper checks sources nightly, updates a plain-text file, and the site rebuilds. Even if you subscribe, your contact info isn’t stored—it’s passed directly to a transactional email/SMS service (like Twilio or Postmark) and deleted after sending.

To test connectivity, the page weighs under 50 KB (loading in <1 second on 2G) and uses no JavaScript for core functionality. Event data is cached for 24 hours, so if the scraper fails, the last known good version stays up. For security, the site is hosted as a static bundle (no server-side code) and served over HTTPS. Example: a Vermont town’s version pulls from their .gov calendar, the high school athletics page, and a volunteer-run Google Sheet—no login required to view or submit events (though submissions route through a moderator’s email to prevent spam).

The trade-off? Less personalization. You can’t save favorite events or get recommendations—but you also can’t be tracked, hacked, or locked out. It’s a bulletin board, not a platform. For small towns, that’s enough: one resident reported checking it weekly and seeing 90% of events they’d previously only heard about via Facebook, without ever logging in.
